The Coalition

The Founding

House Anada spent a thousand years prizing bloodline above all else. That doctrine built one of the most respected Houses in the Empire, and it very nearly ended us — passing the seat to the strongest child produced the Mad Lord Kainir, and a House that would speak to no one had no one left to call on.

What saved it was not blood. Kasmi Anada took the House back with allies: warriors of House Ledria, the last survivor of a family Kainir had gutted, an Anada lord who broke ranks, and finally a traitor within Kainir's own walls. She could not have won as a House. She won as a coalition.

Years later, at Felucia, it happened again. A human admiral held a resistance together in the jungle for months. A captain stayed behind so she could be brought home. Warships built by an outsider's hand broke the Republic fleet. The campaign ended with Felucia permanently Imperial — and with Darth Yuvania gone, without explanation, without a successor named.

Three of those left behind refused to fight over the empty seat.

Darth Car'htizi
Anahkyr. Yuvania's wife.
High Lord Winter
A Dathomirian witch, once of House Ledria. Yuvania's oldest friend.
Lord Viriidia Dotia
Yuvania's sister-apprentice. Last of a bloodline Kainir destroyed.

By the old House's reckoning, not one of them belonged at its table — a Chiss, a witch, and a woman with no house left. They founded the Anada Coalition instead, and simply wrote down what had been true for a generation.

I

Standing

Rank grants nothing on your character sheet. It grants people — which is far more valuable, and is how the Sith have always worked.

A Neophyte can be strong. A Neophyte cannot command anyone, cannot take an apprentice, and cannot make a Lord do anything.

How rank is earned

A Lord who believes a character is ready vouches for them — in character, publicly, naming what that character did. Vouching costs something. A Lord who elevates a fool has elevated a fool, and everyone watched.

Nobody applies. Nobody submits a completed checklist. There are no requirements to satisfy.

To reachWho must agree
AcolyteOne Lord vouches
ApprenticeOne Lord willing to take you — or two Lords vouch, making you a Coalition apprentice
Senior ApprenticeYour Master, or the Lords overseeing a Coalition apprentice
LordYour Master and two Lords — or your Master and one Darth
Above LordLeadership's judgment. These are recognized rather than vouched for.
The Coalition apprentice route matters. A character who has done the work but whom no individual Lord has taken on is not stuck. Two Lords vouch, the Coalition sponsors their training, and any willing Lord or Overseer teaches them. Nobody's progress bottlenecks behind one person's attention.

What gets noticed

  • Doing something memorable when it counted
  • Answering when someone called for help
  • Being reliably present, not only when it's convenient
  • Making other people's scenes better
  • Ambition others can feel — taking territory, taking rivals, taking risks

Showing up is a floor, not a signal. Attendance alone does not make your character matter.

Falling

Rank granted in character can be lost in character. A Lord who is humiliated, outmaneuvered or exposed can be brought down. This is not a punishment mechanic — it is the same engine in reverse, and it is what makes promotion mean anything.

A character who falls keeps every point and every ability. They lost standing. They did not lose their training.

The Character

Twenty points. One point buys +1 to anything. Build the character, not the numbers.

A distinctive character with three proficiencies they rarely roll is worth more than an optimized one nobody remembers.

Defense — pick one

Dodge. Keep AC at 10 or below and invest in Dodge. When an attack beats your AC, roll d20 + Dodge to save yourself.

Armor. Push AC above 10. Attacks that fail to beat it simply miss and you never roll to defend — but you lose dodge entirely.

Decide early

Points in Dodge are wasted the moment your AC exceeds 10. This is the single most common way a new sheet is ruined.

Combat

There is no separate Attack modifier. What you are good at is your proficiency. An attack roll is d20 + the one Combat Proficiency you are using, and nothing else.

Lightsabers +5 → d20 + 5.

Lightsabers · Blades · Polearms · Unarmed · Pistols · Rifles · Heavy Weapons · Explosives · Force

Force is the combat proficiency for offensive Force powers — anything that damages, debuffs or disables. It works exactly as a weapon proficiency does. You may only ever apply one combat proficiency to a roll.

A duelist and a sorcerer are built the same way — by investing in the thing they actually do, rather than paying a shared tax first.

Caps — sourced, not ranked

There are no rank-based caps. A modifier only has to be accounted for.

ModifierWhat it takes
+1 to +3Free. Build what you want, including at creation.
+4 to +6Point to the training — a teacher, a mentor, a formative event in your history, a stretch of RP where you drilled it. It only needs to exist in your character's story.
+7Expert. Granted by leadership in recognition of what your character has done.
+8Mastery. Granted separately and later. Requires Expert in that same proficiency first.
+6 is the ceiling on earned skill. The last two points exist only as gifts, and they are two separate gifts.

Expert says a character is among the best in the Coalition at something. A Mastery says there is no one better — a character at +8 should be able to name the arc that earned it. Both apply identically to combat, non-combat and Knowledge proficiencies.

This is what lets a newcomer arrive as a veteran soldier or a trained physician instead of a blank slate, and it's why rank caps are absent — a character's ceiling should answer to their story, not their title.

Hard limits

Health max 10 · Action Points max 5 · no dodge above 10 AC · one combat proficiency per attack roll.

Approval

Every character sheet is approved by leadership before it is live. That covers new characters and every change afterward — points spent, abilities added, forms progressed, grants recorded.

Approval is checking that the sheet is accounted for: that points spent match points earned, that modifiers above +3 have a source in your character's story, that abilities have a teacher named or a completed self-study arc, and that restricted abilities have a sponsor.

This is a check, not a negotiation over who your character is allowed to be. A sheet is approved or returned with a specific reason — never left sitting.

Base stats — Health, AC, AP, Dodge — rise only through event points. No class teaches you to have more hit points.

Ability classes

Your first three powers in a family each pay +1 point, exactly like the three classes of a saber form. The fourth and beyond in that family grant the power only.

Three classes deep in anything pays. After that you are collecting options.

Each of those three payouts may go to the proficiency the power rolls, or to a thematic secondary — a student deep in Telepathy may build Insight or Mysticism as they prefer.

Retraining

After a significant arc, injury or turn in your story, you may move points to match where your character actually went. The story is the only justification needed.

Pace

Learn at a reasonable pace. If it appears someone is assembling a powerful character rather than playing one, they receive one warning. If it continues, they are removed from the Coalition for no less than seven days and may appeal to the officers.

For officers: this is a judgment call about intent, not a hidden number. A player genuinely obsessed with lightning who RPs it constantly is not the problem case. Record warnings — one line, with the date — so a second strike isn't one officer's memory against a player's.

Declared at Acolyte — early enough to shape a character rather than confirm one. The Discipline comes immediately; the Mastery is granted later, in recognition. A character may change Paths once, through RP, if their story genuinely turns.

Saber Forms

Forms are flavor. All lightsaber combat rolls on the Lightsabers proficiency, and no form changes that. What a form grants is one signature technique — and only to those who have mastered it.

TierWhat it coversGrants
BasicStance, grip, the fundamental strikes. You can name the form and hold it badly.+1
AdvancedApplication under pressure. Where the form fails, and what it fails against.+1
MasteryThe form becomes instinct. You stop thinking about it.+1 & signature

Classes must be separate sessions. No one masters a form in an afternoon. Each class gives one point — put it into Lightsabers, or into the proficiency that form drills. Decide at each class, not once per form.

Only masters may teach, and only up to the tier they hold. A rare form can genuinely die out of the Coalition if nobody carries it.

One signature per encounter, however many forms you have mastered. A duelist mid-fight does not cycle through the catalogue.

VI

The Force

Access is a person, not a number. You may learn anything you can find someone willing to teach you — that is the only gate.

A Neophyte who charms a High Lord into teaching them something dangerous has earned it through play, and that scene is worth more to the Coalition than any tier chart.

Learning a power grants the power. It does not grant a point — beyond the first three in a family, which pay like any training.

Breadth is not power

Powers cluster into families. Learning more powers within a family gives you more things to do — never bigger numbers.

A Sith with nine lightning techniques is a virtuoso with a wide repertoire, not a stronger character than one with a single well-practised bolt.

Without this rule, one family of powers eclipses everything else in the game and every Sith builds the same character.

VII

Restricted Knowledge

Ordinary powers are taught. Restricted powers are survived.

It takes time

No restricted ability is learned in a session. Each is an arc — a sequence of scenes over weeks, with a sponsor, a source, and something standing between you and it. A tomb that has to be reached. A holocron that has to be opened. A ritual that has to be performed correctly the first time.

Three scenes is the floor. Deeper works — Essence Transfer, Tsaiwinokka Hoyakut, Force Walk — should take considerably longer and involve other characters.

It can maim you

The dark side does not hand its deepest techniques over cleanly. A failed attempt, an interrupted ritual, a spirit that resists being bound — any of these can leave a mark.

A failed attempt inflicts an injury as though you had reached 0 HP, rolled and described normally. Injuries from restricted learning do not clear in seven days. They persist until resolved through medical RP, and some should not fully resolve at all.

A withered hand. A voice that no longer sounds like yours. Eyes that stopped being the colour they were. These are not punishments — they are the visible record of what you reached for, and they should be worn.

It can kill you

The deepest works can end a character — but only under conditions the player agreed to before the arc began.

  • The DM states the stakes in advance. Before the first scene, the player is told plainly what failure can cost.
  • The player consents, or the arc doesn't run. A player who wants the power but not the risk is offered a longer, safer path to something lesser. They are never told no and left with nothing.
  • Death arrives through the story, not a die roll. Nobody loses a character to a surprise natural 1.
  • The turning-back point is real. At least once, visibly, the character must be offered a way out — and the player must refuse it knowingly.

No one is required to gamble

Restricted powers are not the only route to being formidable. A Sith with a mastered saber form, a Path Mastery and four proficiencies at +5 is genuinely dangerous and has risked nothing but their dignity.

Leadership should never imply that the deepest powers are the real ones. The moment players feel they must risk a character to keep up, the risk stops being a choice and starts being a tax.

Self-Study

You may always take what you were not offered. Holocrons, stolen texts, a rival's corpse, a spirit that wants something in return.

It begins with a conversation out of character. Before anything is written, you and a DM agree on what is being attempted, where the knowledge comes from, and what it might cost. Nothing starts without it.

Then you write three stories — not three scenes of the same thing, but three distinct stages, each posted separately.

I
The Acquisition

How the knowledge came into your hands. A holocron retrieved. A text found in the archives. A body searched. A spirit that agreed to speak. This story is about reaching the source, and what stood in the way.

II
The Decipherment

Working out how the thing is actually done. Translation, study, failed first attempts, a diagram that makes no sense until it suddenly does. This is the story most players want to skip, and the one that makes a self-taught character believable.

III
The Practice

Putting it to use — and learning the cost in character. What the ability takes out of you is discovered here, not announced beforehand. The burn, the bleed, the thing that looks back. Your character finds out the same way the reader does.

Then the DM confirms the ability is learned, or that it isn't yet.

Any open ability can be self-studied. Some restricted abilities can, but not all — which ones is a DM call, made during that first conversation. The deepest works stay closed to anyone without a sponsor.

Self-study pays no more than a class

The points are identical. What it gives you instead is the ability to learn something without being approved to do it — and a character with three chapters a classroom would never have produced. Nobody should take the solitary route expecting a mechanical edge. They should take it because their character would.

You describe the wound — how it happened, what it looks like, what it costs you to move. Only the location is out of your hands.

Injuries last seven days or until resolved through medical RP. They stack. Penalties may take a stat below zero. Healed wounds become scars on your dossier.

The Death Stake

Any player may choose, for their own character, that 0 HP means death. Opt-in, honored by DMs, and carrying no mechanical reward — that is the entire point. The risk is real and uncompensated.

Nobody should be pressured toward it. Nobody should treat it as the serious way to play.
X

Masters & Apprentices

A Master teaches — abilities, forms, culture, and the judgment to use them.

Apprenticeship runs both directions. An apprentice reflects on their Master. One who is hot-headed, starts drama, or is visibly weak puts their Master in hot water. A weak Master colors how their apprentice is seen, however capable that apprentice is.

There are no mechanics here. The reputational weight is the mechanic, and it should be played.

Anyone may seek a different Master. Advancement must never bottleneck through one person. If a Lord ignores a player, another can take them on.

XI

Recognition

The risk in a system without checklists is that quiet players go unseen. Three habits, running in parallel, covering each other's blind spots.

The appreciation channel

Public shoutouts for good scenes. Leadership posts first and often — these channels die in two weeks if nobody models the habit. Ask for specifics, not praise: "Vaska stayed behind to cover the retreat and made my escape mean something" tells you everything; "great scene with Vaska" tells you nothing. It will over-index on the socially confident. That's fine. It's one input.

Attendance

A flag, not a reward. It tells leadership who to go look at. If it were itself a path to advancement, we would have rebuilt the checklist.

Monthly review

Leadership asks, every month: who has been around without a promotion, a Master, or a story of their own? Usually it means they don't know how to start one, not that they're unwilling. Reach out.

Watch for two kinds of noticing. The spectacular moment during an event is easy. The character who is always there, who answers when help is called, who makes other people's scenes work — that one never spikes, and it is the one that gets missed.

The most important habit in this system

A checklist at least tells a shy player what to do next. We removed that, so leadership fills the gap deliberately — and sets the example rather than waiting to be shown one.

Standing is what we think of you. Capability is what you can do.

Neither one gates the other, and that is the whole design.
